The Siege of Uxellodunum
#########################

What History says
=============================

( Source: https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Si%C3%A8ge_d%27Uxellodunum )

One year after the surrender of Vercingetorix at Alesia, Caesar and his main lieutenants are busy pacifying the last pockets of resistance that remain here and there across the region, in 51 BC.

In the south-west, two Gaulish chiefs, **Lucterius** and **Drappes**, followed by a few thousand men and pursued by the legate **Gaius Caninius Rebilus**, take refuge in the stronghold of Uxellodunum, in the heart of the territory of the Cadurci (in present-day Quercy).
The legate then divides his two legions into three widely spaced camps, on the heights overlooking the oppidum.

The Gauls trapped inside the oppidum, some of whom had known the harshness of famine during the disastrous siege of Alesia (where Lucterius had been present), then set out to procure food and provisions. Lucterius and Drappes leave a garrison of about 2,000 men safely inside Uxellodunum, taking the rest of their force (a few thousand men) to forage for supplies. Over several days, they manage to gather a large quantity of victuals in the surrounding Cadurcian countryside, while regularly attacking the Roman fortified positions to cover their sortie.

Caninius Rebilus, learning of the attempt, then deploys part of his forces against the convoy, and scatters it, inflicting many casualties and taking many captives. In the struggle, Lucterius flees, and Drappes, whose camp was not far away, is captured.
Returning to his encircling positions, Caninius Rebilus then has the siege works around the stronghold intensified. With the reinforcements that have arrived in the meantime, the Roman forces now amount to a little over four legions, enough to surround it completely.

**Caesar**, meanwhile, learns that Caninius Rebilus is facing the revolt of the Cadurci and the Senones. He sets out to punish the rebellion in person, so as to crush any further inclination to rebel. Crossing the region at lightning speed, at the head of his cavalry, and followed further behind by two legions on foot, he arrives at Uxellodunum.

Faced with the natural walls of the stronghold, and with the considerable reserves of the besieged, Caesar decides not to launch a frontal assault. He undertakes to cut the rebels off from their water supply; and has tunnels dug to approach the main spring from underground.

The besieged react by attempting to set fire to the whole Roman siege works: they fill barrels with tallow, pitch and dry wood, set them alight and roll them down onto the Romans' earthworks. But that is not enough. Soon, the Roman army dries up the spring, and the besieged, compelled by thirst, surrender.

Caesar accepts the surrender of the stronghold, and draws on his reputation as a man of great clemency to deal with the vanquished. He chooses to punish the captives by cutting off their hands, while sparing their lives, so as to discourage the Gauls from ever rebelling again.
Drappes, a captive, refuses food for several days and dies. Meanwhile, Lucterius takes refuge with an aristocrat named Epasnactus, who will hand him over as a prisoner to Caesar.

What Legend says
=======================================

Popular tradition tells that a band of warriors from the far reaches of Armorica (present-day Brittany), had come to lend a hand to the Gaulish chiefs Lucterius and Drappes.

Among them were a handful of druids, who used their powers to heal, invigorate and counsel the insurgents during the siege of Uxellodunum.

When the natural citadel fell, these druids fled to a nearby cave, with a few survivors. And in despair, they resorted to a forbidden magic, sacrificing their lives so that the soul of the Gaulish people might survive the inexorable advance of the Roman legions.

Many versions exist and contradict each other as to the content of this invocation. But all of them mention {% symbol "Toutatis" for "nom_du_dieu_sanguinaire" %}, the warrior god and father of the Gaulish people; as well as a set of artefacts - among them Roman weapons - which supposedly served as receptacles for this ritual.

A descendant of the knight **Bertrand du Guesclin** (14th century) boasted, in his memoirs, of having had custody of what he calls the **"Druids' Last Wish"**.

Perhaps the museum pieces displayed here at the Cromlech really are linked to this legend - who knows?
